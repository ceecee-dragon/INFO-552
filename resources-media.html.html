My K-8 school librarian once said, "If you plan to not return a book on purpose, that is stealing and stealing is wrong." I'll let you imagine what I asked to prompt such a response.

Because I loved her so much and couldn't bear disappointing her, I waited until the last day of the school year to steal a book she checked out to me every week for at least two years. I walked into the library, pretended to read my fave book, and walked out with my book. I have no doubt she knew it was gone, and I'm sure she knew I took it. We never spoke about it—not when our paths crossed years later as professionals. (I ran into her as a high school student and a few times after, when I became an educator.)

I don't think she knew, or perhaps she did, the desperation of a girl like me needing to see herself in a story. Perhaps only a librarian truly understands the urgency for marginalized kids to find a mirror in a world of windows. Because I'll tell you something: in my K-12 experience, only four teachers—all women—ever made me feel like I mattered. The rest saw me as good for one thing, or destined for nothing because of my gender. I think a lot of my students and some of you can relate. One of the most meaningful lessons my school librarian taught me was the idea that literacy, books— aren't just for learning; they are lifelines. So while I can't in good conscience condone theft, I do support checking out the books/media below.

Sulwe by Lupita Nyong'o

This children's picture book is a quiet revelation. Nyong’o dismantles colorism with tenderness and truth, giving children and adults the language to love their skin and the beauty they have within - a tale of learning to love yourself. A must read for every girl.

The night sky doesn’t ask to be lighter — neither should you. Let your melanin pop 'n glow like moonshine.
Brave New Girl by Rachel Vincent (series)

In this YA dystopia, human worth is reduced to genetic sameness. Vincent’s heroine, a mutant in the system, rebukes conformity. The pacing is crisp and the stakes are urgent; under the cloning sheen lies a feminist edge: difference is not a defect but weapon.

In a world that demands sameness, dare to be the change.

Lo’s retelling replaces the glass slipper with boots and the prince with a huntress. In short, this is queer Cinderella version we desperately needed. Darkly romantic and steeped in faerie lore, this coming-of-age story keeps desire without erasing selfhood. For teens and adults tired of fairy tales that flatten their heroines, “Ash” offers teeth and tenderness: love that enlarges rather than consumes.

Cinderella leveled up and traded a prince for a huntress. Cindy got game.

And the second book I ever thought about stealing was part of this series. Again, I don't condone theft, but titular character Anita is the Femme Fatale ideal personified. She is fierce, flawed, and perfectly unladylike in all her adventures. and sexuality. For readers seeking a heroine allowed to take up space in every domain, Anita's your girl. I'm gonna do my due diligence and say this is definitely adult reading.

She slays, she sins, she survives.

Women Dont Riot by Ana Castillo

Women don’t riot, not in maquilas in Malaysia, Mexico, or Korea, not in sweatshops in New York or El Paso. They don’t revolt in kitchens, laundries, or nurseries. Not by the hundreds or thousands, changing sheets in hotels or in laundries when scalded by hot water, not in restaurants where they clean and clean and clean their hands raw. Women don’t riot, not sober and earnest, or high and strung out, not of any color, any race, not the rich, poor, or those in between. And mothers of all kinds especially don’t run rampant through the streets. In college those who’ve thought it out join hands in crucial times, carry signs, are dragged away in protest. We pass out petitions, organize a civilized vigil, return to work the next day. We women are sterilized, have more children than they can feed, don’t speak the official language, want things they see on TV, would like to own a TV— women who were molested as children raped, beaten, harassed, which means every last one sooner or later; women who’ve defended themselves and women who can’t or don’t know how we don’t—won’t ever rise up in arms. We don’t storm through cities, take over the press, make a unified statement, once and for all: A third-millennium call— from this day on no more, not me, not my daughter, not her daughter either. Women don’t form a battalion, march arm in arm across continents bound by the same tongue, same food or lack thereof, same God, same abandonment, same broken heart, raising children on our own, have so much endless misery in common that must stop not for one woman or every woman, but for the sake of us all. Quietly, instead, one and each takes the offense, rejection, bureaucratic dismissal, disease that should not have been, insult, shove, blow to the head, a knife at her throat. She won’t fight, she won’t even scream— taught as she’s been to be brought down as if by surprise. She’ll die like an ant beneath a passing heel. Today it was her. Next time who.

Clothes don't make a person; they do make it easier to express ourselves though. Jacob's Dress is an affirming picture book that handles gender nonconformity with care. Perfect for kids of all ages to get thinking about how you want to present yourself to the world.

For the pink boys, tomboys, and everything else Femme, wear your best outfit like a suit of armor and conquer your world!

Do I still identify with Jong’s Isadora? Some days. Decades after that first read, and this story still lives rent free in the front of my mind. The person I am on the inside, is that the person I show the world? What is holding me back, keeping me from doing and saying the things that need to be said? Not just in my professional life but my love life too.

There's so much fun in being a woman once you recognize the world can be your queendom.

As an aside. Here I am decades later and I still have the book I stole. All the librarians out there, pay attention. There is nada like school & library book binding. It is 2025 and this jawn is still intact. This book has been with me through the loss of my virginity, every girl I've kissed, all the times I snuck back into the house - and once stepped on that book to muffle the sound of my footsteps, and it'll probably still be around (and readable) when we humans go extinct.
